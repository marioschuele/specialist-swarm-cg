# BTS-Synthetic Firm Voice (transformation-led)

This is a fictional house style for a hackathon exercise. It is not an official
statement or style guide of any real company. Do not attribute it to a real firm.

## Core idea

Every proposal answers one question: **what changes for the customer's business,
and how do we know it will pay off?** Lead with the outcome and the business
case. Technology and contract detail support that story; they never open it.

## Tone

- Confident, plain, and specific. Short sentences. Active voice.
- Speak to the customer's goals ("you", "your teams"), not about our product.
- Business-case first: tie every capability to a measurable outcome
  (cost, time-to-insight, risk, revenue).
- Honest about trade-offs. Concede where a competitor is stronger and pivot
  to where the customer wins. Credibility beats polish.

## Structure rules

1. **Open with the outcome.** The executive summary has three bullets, each in
   the form "Outcome - how - evidence".
2. **"What this means for you."** End each major section with a one- or
   two-sentence block under this exact heading, translating the section into
   customer impact.
3. **Lead with total cost of ownership,** not headline price, in the commercial
   section. Show a multi-year view when the specialists provide one.
4. **Frame contract points as shared risk management,** not as demands. State
   our position, the reason, and the proposed compromise.
5. **Close with a clear next step** (who does what by when). Do not invent
   dates the RFP does not give.

## Signature phrasing (use sparingly, adapt to context)

- "What this means for you: ..."
- "The business case rests on ..."
- "We would measure success by ..."
- "Where [competitor] is strong is ...; where you gain is ..."
- "Here is how we would de-risk this together."

## Avoid

- Unsupported superlatives ("best-in-class", "revolutionary", "world-leading").
- Claims or numbers that the specialists did not provide. Never invent
  customer names, references, benchmarks, or market statistics.
- Negative statements about competitors beyond what the competitive-intel
  specialist supplied. No disparagement.
- Jargon without a business consequence attached.
- Statements about real companies, real clients, or anything that reads as an
  official corporate position.
- Internal-only content in the client document: discount floors, walk-away
  positions, and internal approval rules stay out of the customer-facing text.

## Self-check before finalising

- Does the first paragraph state an outcome, not a feature?
- Does every major section end with "What this means for you"?
- Is every number traceable to a specialist's output or the RFP?
- Would the customer's CFO and CIO each find their question answered?
